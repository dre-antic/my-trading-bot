Call her every morning at the time she is normally up, and line up one person who lives near her to knock if she does not answer. That is the check that fits a pendant she will not wear, a smartphone she does not use, no cameras, and a budget of about $100. It tells you the two things you named when they show up as "she is not answering": she did not get up, or she cannot get to the phone.

Keep the $100 until that is in place. A sensor can add a morning text after she has agreed to it. Nothing in this budget reliably texts you within minutes of a fall.

## Why this fits her, and you

You are three hours away. An alert only helps if someone closer can walk over. A device she has to charge, wear, or press after she is on the floor will fail on the day you need it. A phone she already answers works the other way around: her voice means she is up, and silence is the alarm.

Tell her the arrangement in plain words. "I will call at 8:30. If you do not pick up, Jane will knock." She can say yes to that. A box she did not agree to is the thing most likely to be unplugged, and an unplugged sensor can look like a quiet morning.

## What to set up this week

1. Pick the time she is reliably up. Put a repeating alarm on your phone: "Call Mom. Two tries, then call Jane."
2. Ask her who nearby she would want at the door. Agree with that person that they call her local emergency number if she does not come to the door and something seems wrong. You getting in the car is the backup, because three hours is a long time to be on the floor.
3. Call or text the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 and ask whether her county has a telephone reassurance program. These are free daily calls run through the local Area Agency on Aging. Where they exist, an unanswered call is passed to the person she named, and sometimes to a welfare check. Her county may not have one, so ask rather than counting on it. On the same call, ask about home-delivered meals. Where she would accept them, a driver at the door most days is a stronger check than a phone call, and there is often a wait.
4. On your next visit, leave a handset where a fall is likely: the bedside table, and the bathroom if a cord will reach. A fall she can still call out from is a different problem from a phone left in the kitchen.

A second call in the late afternoon cuts the worst gap. With one morning call, a fall at 3 p.m. can sit until the next morning. With a second call, that stretch is the time until late afternoon. That is still hours, and it is the honest limit of a phone she has to be able to reach.

## The only hardware worth buying, and only after the calls exist

If she has home Wi-Fi and she agrees, add a Tapo H100 hub and a Tapo T100 motion sensor. On the Tapo US store on Nov 20, 2026 the sale prices were $17.99 and $12.99 (regular $22.99 and $19.99), about $31 before tax and shipping. Free shipping on that store starts at $49. The sensor needs the hub. The hub needs a wall outlet and her 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, and it has to stay plugged in. Alerts go to the free Tapo app on your phone. Leave Tapo Care off. That subscription is for camera video. She never needs the app.

Mount the sensor in the kitchen, or in the hall she walks on the way to the bathroom, around waist to chest height, aimed across her path. Keep it off heaters, sunny windows, and anywhere a pet walks in the morning. A cat through the kitchen looks like she is up.

In the app, make two automations and limit both to a morning window, such as 7:00 to 10:30. Tapo's Smart Actions guide, updated Nov 18, 2026, lists "Motion Detected" and "No Motion Detected for a Set Time" as triggers, an effective time window, and "Send Me Notifications" as the action.

- Motion: notify you, "Mom is up."
- No motion for the longest quiet time the app allows: notify you, "Kitchen has been quiet. Call Mom."

Set the action to a notification on your phone. Leave the hub chime and siren off so the box stays quiet in her house. The sensor's battery is a coin cell; Tapo rates it at up to about two years and the app can warn you when it is low. Turn that warning on.

Test it the day you install it, before you trust it.

- Walk through the room and confirm "Mom is up" arrives on your phone.
- Stay out of the room long enough to confirm the quiet alert also arrives.
- Unplug the hub and see whether the app tells you it is offline. If a dead hub looks the same as a quiet kitchen, the phone call stays the check you act on.
- If she is willing, have her stay in the bedroom past her usual time once. Some apps fire the quiet alert only at the moment the timer runs out. If that moment was the middle of the night, outside your morning window, you will get no morning alert on the day she never gets up. If that test fails, use the sensor only as the "she's up" ping, and keep acting on the phone call.

## What would change this

I would add a fall sensor only if she has looked at it, understands it has no lens, and still wants it. The one that fits the hardware budget is the Aqara Presence Sensor FP2, listed at $82.99 on Aqara's US store, with no monthly fee. It joins Wi-Fi on its own, so there is no second hub. Aqara's manual and product page put hard limits on it: fall detection needs it mounted on the ceiling; at a 2.8 meter ceiling the fall area is about a 2 meter radius; it can miss a slow slide down a wall; a falling or shaking object can set it off; in that mode the zone and presence features turn off, so it stops being your "did she get up" check; and Aqara says it is not a medical device and is only for notification. A small bathroom is the room that matches that circle. A white box on the ceiling is also easy to mistake for a camera, which she has already ruled out.

The purpose-built version of the same idea does not fit the budget. Silvie's shop lists its radar at $150 per room plus a $44 monthly membership, and the page says that without the membership the sensor cannot notify anyone. One sensor covers one room, it needs 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, and a fall alert is a push notification in their app. It does not call emergency services. Bedroom, bathroom, and living room, which is what they suggest, is $399 plus that monthly fee.

I set aside pendants, cameras, and bed pads that page a caregiver only inside the house. A smart plug on the coffee maker also loses: if she never turns the kettle on, the plug stays silent, and silence from a broken plug looks the same.

## Watch out for

This is a morning safety check between you, her, and one neighbor. It is not a monitoring service and it is not medical care. If you believe she is on the floor, hurt, or not breathing, call her local emergency number.

A motion sensor reports a quiet room. It does not know the difference between a fall, a nap, and a long chapter of a book, so leave the quiet alert inside the morning window.

## I'll assume these unless you tell me otherwise
